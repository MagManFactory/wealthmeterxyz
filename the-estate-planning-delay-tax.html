LONGFORM · ESTATE STRUCTURE AND LATER-LIFE MOBILITY

The Estate Planning Delay Tax

OCTOBER 27, 2026 · WEALTHMETER

A household can postpone estate documents for years while its accounts, beneficiaries, and decision makers change. The eventual cost may appear as a missed choice or a delay in access, even when no federal estate tax is due.

“Delay tax” is a planning metaphor, not a government levy with a fixed rate. The exposure depends on account terms, state law, family structure, and whether the owner can still make decisions. The first question concerns authority during life: who can manage money if the owner cannot act?

Authority must exist before it is needed

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau explains that a power of attorney appoints an agent to act for another person. A durable financial power of attorney generally remains effective during incapacity. The document requires careful selection of the agent and an understanding of the powers granted. Financial institutions may have their own review process, and state law governs execution and scope.

When no usable authority is in place, a family may have to seek a court-supervised route to manage assets. The procedure and expense vary by jurisdiction; no national cost estimate follows from that possibility. Preparing authority while the owner can direct the choice retains options and allows a discussion of safeguards.

Authority creates risk as well as continuity. The CFPB notes that an agent may misuse access, including making gifts or changing beneficiaries. Its guides for financial caregivers describe fiduciary responsibilities and practical record keeping. A household can specify who receives account statements, where records are stored, and when another trusted person should be consulted. The appropriate safeguards depend on the person, assets, and legal form.

Account instructions can move on a separate track

A will is one part of a transfer plan. The SEC Investor.gov describes transfer-on-death registration for eligible securities accounts, which can direct those holdings to named recipients without probate. The FDIC describes payable-on-death bank accounts as deposit arrangements with named beneficiaries. Availability, implementation, and consequences depend on the account and governing law.

A review should compare current account registrations and beneficiary instructions with the intended recipients in the estate documents. Marriage, divorce, death, a new child, or a change in care obligations can make an old designation inconsistent with current wishes. Changing one document does not necessarily update every account instruction. The institutions' current records determine what is on file.

These account instructions can simplify transfer, but the owner should check whether they coordinate with a trust or other estate document. The SEC's explanation concerns securities held in an eligible transfer-on-death registration. The FDIC's payable-on-death discussion concerns deposits. Neither source establishes the treatment of a household's home, closely held business, retirement plan, or property in every state. A complete map names the legal owner and current recipient for each major asset category.

Federal tax exposure requires a separate calculation

For decedents dying in 2026, the IRS Form 706 instructions state a $15 million basic federal estate-tax exclusion. The applicable calculation can be affected by lifetime gifts and other rules. Many households will have no federal estate-tax liability. State estate or inheritance taxes may still matter, and the rules depend on the jurisdiction.

For a married household with material exposure, an executor may need to consider an election to transfer unused exclusion to a surviving spouse. The IRS instructions describe a timely Form 706 as the standard route and a limited late-election procedure for eligible estates. That is a filing decision after death with deadlines; it is not a reason to assign a federal tax bill to every family that delays planning.

The 2026 exclusion is a federal threshold, not a safe harbor for every transfer issue. The IRS filing test considers the gross estate together with adjusted taxable gifts and specified exemptions. Portability requires attention to a deceased spouse's estate return even when that estate would otherwise fall below the filing threshold. A qualified tax adviser can decide whether the election matters and whether a deadline remains open.

Review the current map

A useful review lists major accounts, ownership, named beneficiaries, insurance and retirement designations, financial authority, and the person who can locate documents. It then identifies conflicts and missing decisions. Date the review and repeat it after a change in household membership, asset ownership, or residence. A lawyer familiar with the relevant state can assess execution, probate, trusts, and tax exposure.

Delay matters most when it removes the owner's ability to direct the choice. A current beneficiary record, a usable financial authority document, and an accessible asset inventory can reduce uncertainty for the people who must act. Their value depends on being valid, consistent, and findable when needed.
