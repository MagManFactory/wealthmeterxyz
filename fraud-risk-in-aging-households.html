LONGFORM · COMPENSATION, GOVERNANCE, AND HOUSEHOLD EXPOSURE

Fraud Risk in Aging Households

OCTOBER 25, 2026 · WEALTHMETER

A caller claims that an account is under attack and asks its owner to move savings to a protective account. The transfer itself is the loss event. A short verification pause can change what happens next.

Fraud risk is an operating problem for a household balance sheet. An attacker needs a plausible story, an urgent decision, and a payment channel. The victim may have full financial capacity and still respond to a convincing impersonation. A useful defense therefore focuses on transaction workflow and independent verification, without assuming that age alone explains vulnerability.

Read the reported losses correctly

In its October 2026 report, the Federal Trade Commission says adults age 60 and over reported about $3 billion in fraud losses for 2025, compared with about $600 million for 2020. The increase reflects reports submitted to the agency, not a complete count of all losses or a measured probability for every older person. Changes in reporting and the concentration of very large losses affect interpretation.

The FTC also reports that older adults filed loss reports at lower rates than younger adults, while the median reported individual loss was higher. Those findings can coexist. They argue against a simple claim that every older person is more likely to be scammed. They do show why a single successful transfer can matter greatly to retirement reserves with limited time to rebuild them.

Interrupt the transfer path

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau warns that real government agencies and financial institutions do not ask someone to move money to protect it. A demand for secrecy, a sudden deadline, or instructions to send funds through cryptocurrency, gift cards, cash, or a new account should trigger independent verification. End the conversation, then call the institution through a number already known or found on an official statement or website.

This pause is most useful before authorization. Once money leaves, recovery may depend on payment method, timing, and the institution's ability to intervene. Households can agree in advance that an unfamiliar payee or a large transfer requires a separate conversation with a trusted person. That is a decision rule, not a guarantee of prevention.

Give the right person the right role

The CFPB distinguishes informal help, a trusted contact, account access arrangements, and power of attorney. A trusted contact can be notified when an institution suspects exploitation. That designation alone gives the contact no authority to withdraw money or trade assets. A power of attorney can grant transaction authority and requires more careful selection, documentation, and oversight.

Review which accounts permit a trusted contact, keep that person's details current, and record who may review statements or act on the owner's behalf. CFPB planning guidance urges families to clarify authority before illness or diminished capacity makes a rushed decision more likely. A helper's access should match the task and the owner's preferences. Broad account access creates its own exploitation risk.

Respond promptly if money moved

Contact the bank, credit union, card issuer, or brokerage through its verified channel and explain the suspected transaction. Ask what the institution can do immediately, preserve statements and messages, and change compromised credentials through the institution's own site. The CFPB reporting guide also points families to Adult Protective Services and law enforcement when exploitation is suspected. The CFPB scam guide identifies the FTC and FBI Internet Crime Complaint Center as reporting channels.

The practical household control is a documented pause before an unfamiliar transfer, paired with a contact plan that does not hand over more authority than necessary. It cannot eliminate deception, but it gives the owner and financial institution time to test the story.
