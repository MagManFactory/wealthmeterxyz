That “average” 401(k) balance may be misleading you

Vanguard reported a $167,970 average balance for 2025, but the participant in the middle had $44,115.

Vanguard’s 2026 retirement-plan figures put the average account balance at $167,970 and the median at $44,115. Both describe 2025 accounts in its dataset. They are not competing estimates of what the typical American has saved.

The average adds the balances and divides by the number of accounts. The median marks the middle of the distribution. Larger balances pull the average upward; here it is about 3.8 times the median. Vanguard reported increases in both measures from the previous year.

The population matters. Its research concerns participants in the plans it administers, not everyone in the country, including workers without an employer retirement plan. An account balance is also not necessarily a person’s total retirement wealth: they may have accounts elsewhere or other income and assets.

Those distinctions make a headline balance a weak personal target. A younger worker with a recently opened account and someone approaching retirement have different planning problems. The same dollar amount does not answer both.

For a hypothetical reader, Amira, 46, the average has become a source of anxiety. Before deciding that she is far behind, she can list the accounts she actually holds, any pension income she expects, her contributions and the time she plans to keep working. That does not settle whether she has enough, but it gives her a more relevant starting point than one national-looking number.

She can then assess her expected spending and the risks around her plan. Comparing one workplace account with someone else’s total savings would obscure rather than clarify that decision.

The Vanguard figures are useful evidence about retirement plans. They show why the word “average” needs care. Neither the mean nor the median supplies a personalized retirement budget, and neither becomes a guarantee that a particular balance is adequate.
