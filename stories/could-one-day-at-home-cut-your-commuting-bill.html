Spending · Practical decision · 2 min

Could one day at home cut your commuting bill?

For Renee, driving less saves money. The size of the saving depends on the journey and what the alternative costs.

Renee, 52, drives 30 miles each way to work on 22 days a month. Her car averages 25 miles per gallon. She is considering a regular work-from-home day. Renee is hypothetical, and the mileage, fuel economy and prices in this example are assumptions.

Her monthly commute is 1,320 miles, requiring 52.8 gallons. At an assumed $4.40 a gallon, fuel costs $232.32. If the price were $3.20, the same journey would cost $168.96. The $63.36 difference comes from price alone; Renee has not driven farther.

Suppose her employer permits a pattern that reduces driving days by a fifth. At the $4.40 assumption, that avoids about $46.46 in monthly fuel spending. The actual saving will vary with the calendar and trips she makes on the days at home.

Fuel is only one line in the budget. Parking and tolls may change with commuting days; some ownership costs will not. A train, carpool or other alternative has its own price and schedule. Buying a new vehicle to lower fuel spending would require a much wider comparison.

Time belongs beside the cash calculation. Renee can estimate the hours the journey takes and what a change would mean for her work and household. That does not assign a universal dollar value to commuting time; it lets her judge something that the fuel receipt cannot show.

Before revising the budget, she needs an arrangement she can actually use. Employer permission, transport availability and the nature of her job determine the options. A saving calculated from a work-from-home day is not real if the day is unavailable.

Renee’s first useful comparison is a normal month under each workable option, including all costs that change. That is more informative than a national fuel-price headline, and it lets her update the calculation when her own price or travel pattern changes.
