Retirement fragility and household risk · October 5, 2026

Long-Term Care as a Balance-Sheet Risk

A household can have a paid-off home and a substantial retirement account, then face a care bill that arrives every month. The assets exist, but the bill requires cash on a schedule. Long-term care planning starts with that mismatch between the size of a balance sheet and the resources that can fund care when it begins.

Long-term care · liquidity · insurance · Medicaid · household planning

Long-term services and supports help a person with activities such as bathing, dressing, eating, and moving through the day. Care may be provided at home, in a community setting, or in a facility. The Administration for Community Living estimates that nearly 70% of people turning 65 will need some support during their remaining years, while about one-third may never need it and roughly 20% will need support for more than five years. Those population estimates do not predict one household's duration, setting, or cost.

The planning variable is the unfunded care obligation over time: expected care payments minus benefits and reliable income, matched against assets that can actually be used when bills arrive.

Medicare leaves a major funding gap

Medicare states that it does not pay for most long-term custodial care, including help with everyday personal tasks at home or in a nursing facility. Coverage for qualifying skilled nursing facility care is a different benefit with different conditions. A household that assumes its Medicare card covers years of personal care may discover the gap after a transition has already been forced.

Medicaid can cover qualifying long-term services and supports, including some home and community-based services, but eligibility and covered services depend on the state and the person's circumstances. Federal Medicaid guidance describes income and resource rules, protections for a spouse, and restrictions on certain asset transfers before an application. The exact treatment of a home, income, trust, or transfer requires state-specific advice. A balance-sheet plan should identify Medicaid as a possible pathway with conditions, never as automatic coverage.

Run a timed stress test

Consider a hypothetical care need costing $75,000 a year for three years. The gross bill would be $225,000 before any change in prices, care intensity, taxes, or existing living costs. This is a stress-test assumption, not a national cost estimate or a prediction. Now place the first year's $75,000 on a calendar. Which account can pay it without a forced home sale or an expensive retirement withdrawal? Which resources can support years two and three if care intensifies?

Separate assets into cash and short-term reserves, retirement accounts, property, and contingent resources such as insurance. A house may carry large equity but still require months to sell, may be needed by a spouse, and may incur borrowing or transaction costs if tapped. Retirement assets can be more liquid on paper while withdrawals raise taxes and reduce the capital supporting later retirement. An adult child's unpaid time can lower cash spending while reducing that child's earnings or capacity to work. The household cost includes those transfers even if no invoice records them.

Insurance changes the exposure, within limits

Private long-term care insurance may cover specified services after a policy's conditions are met. The National Association of Insurance Commissioners advises buyers to inspect the benefit amount, benefit period, elimination period, covered settings, exclusions, and inflation protection. Premiums can change under policy and state rules, and coverage available to one applicant may differ from another's. The relevant comparison is the net cash exposure under the actual contract, including what the household pays before benefits start and after they end.

A family that does not buy coverage still needs an explicit reserve and care decision path. Identify who could coordinate care, where records and policy documents live, what level of help can be provided safely at home, and which assets would fund paid support first. WealthMeter's financial runway tool can clarify the timing of available cash. Its net-worth tool shows the asset stock. The two views should be read together.

Set the decision before the care transition

The next useful step is to obtain local quotes and build a household-specific scenario. Price plausible home care and facility options in the area, verify existing policy terms, and check the state's Medicaid pathway before assuming an asset will remain protected or available. A planner or elder-law attorney can test the tax, benefit, and spousal consequences of a proposed funding sequence. The balance sheet has to survive the care schedule while preserving the other household member's housing and income.

Sources and method

Administration for Community Living, projected need and duration of long-term care.

Medicare, long-term care coverage.

Medicaid, eligibility policy and long-term services rules.
