LONGFORM · COMPENSATION AND HOUSEHOLD EXPOSURE

Deferred Compensation and the Counterparty Illusion

OCTOBER 22, 2026 · WEALTHMETER

An executive may see a deferred compensation balance beside retirement accounts in a benefits portal. That display can obscure the legal difference. In a common nonqualified arrangement, the balance represents a promise by the employer to pay later, and the employee bears the employer's credit risk until payment.

The plan document controls the answer. Some deferred compensation arrangements are qualified retirement plans with separate statutory protections. This article concerns U.S. nonqualified deferred compensation, particularly unfunded arrangements often offered to management or highly compensated employees. The Department of Labor describes top hat plans as unfunded deferred compensation for a select group. Their terms and protections differ from those of an ordinary qualified retirement account.

Follow the claim through financial distress

Deferral postpones receipt of compensation. It does not necessarily move assets into an account owned by the employee. The Internal Revenue Service explains that an employer can use a rabbi trust while preserving the unfunded tax treatment when the trust's assets remain available to the employer's general creditors. An actual plan may use different arrangements, so the trust agreement and plan document deserve review.

If the employer becomes insolvent, a participant in an unfunded plan may hold an unsecured claim under the plan terms. A promise to credit investment returns does not itself create ownership of the referenced investments. The 2026 financial statements of one public issuer expressly describe its nonqualified deferred compensation as an unfunded, unsecured liability and its plan assets as available to general creditors in bankruptcy. That disclosure illustrates the structure; it does not establish the terms of every employer plan.

Tax deferral comes with payment constraints

Section 409A governs many nonqualified deferred compensation arrangements. The IRS lists permitted payment events, including a specified time or schedule, separation from service, disability, death, change of control, and a qualifying unforeseeable emergency. Elections and later changes follow technical rules. A participant usually cannot treat the balance as an on-demand emergency fund simply because it appears on a portal.

The tax consequence of a compliance failure can be substantial. IRS Publication 5528 explains that affected vested amounts can become includible in current income, with an additional 20 percent tax and an interest-based charge. That is a penalty framework, not a projected cost for a compliant plan. Employment taxes can follow different timing rules from income taxes. The plan administrator and a qualified tax adviser should confirm the specific arrangement before an election or payout change.

Employer concentration extends beyond equity

Compensation, career prospects, company stock, and a deferred pay claim can all depend on one employer. A financial shock to the employer may coincide with job loss, a decline in company shares, and pressure on the deferred promise. Those risks are correlated even when the portal shows separate line items. The relevant household measure is the combined exposure to that counterparty, with liquid assets held outside it counted separately.

Deferring pay can still be useful when the tax timing, current marginal rate, future income pattern, and employer risk justify it. The expected tax benefit should be compared with the cost of giving up current liquidity and accepting a concentrated unsecured claim. No fixed percentage limit follows from the public sources; the decision depends on contract terms and the household's ability to absorb a delay or loss.

Read the documents before the election

Ask whether the plan is funded or unfunded, who owns any trust assets, what happens in insolvency, which payment events apply, and whether employment termination changes the schedule. Check beneficiary terms, investment-crediting rules, administrative fees, and the treatment of a merger. Read the employer's financial disclosures when available. A rabbi trust can improve administration without removing general-creditor exposure.

Then stress the household cash plan. Identify obligations due before the deferred balance is scheduled to pay and maintain reserves that do not depend on the same employer. If the employer's credit quality deteriorates, a locked distribution schedule may limit the participant's options. The central question is whether the tax timing benefit compensates for a claim that can weaken at the same time as employment income.
