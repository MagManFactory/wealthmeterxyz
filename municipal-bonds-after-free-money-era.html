Municipal Bonds After the Free-Money Era

A tax-exempt coupon can look attractive when cash earns a meaningful yield. The bond still has a price, a repayment source, and a date on which the issuer may end the payments.

Municipal bonds finance state and local obligations and projects. Their interest is often exempt from federal income tax, which can make them useful in a taxable account. That tax treatment does not fix a bond's market value or ensure payment. After the period when investors could treat low-yield cash as a negligible comparison, a municipal purchase needs a full after-tax comparison with Treasury securities, taxable bonds, and the cash needed soon. The right comparison uses the same horizon and a comparable risk profile.

Start with the household's tax position

IRS Publication 550 says interest on qualifying state and local government obligations is generally excluded from federal income tax. State treatment depends on the issuer, the investor's residence, and applicable law. Some private activity bond interest can be relevant to the alternative minimum tax. A sale before maturity can create a taxable capital gain or loss even when the periodic interest is federally tax exempt. A municipal bond fund can distribute income with different tax characteristics from an individual bond.

A tax-equivalent yield is a screening calculation. It cannot predict an investor’s realized return. Divide a tax-exempt yield by one minus the relevant marginal federal tax rate when making a federal-only comparison. In a purely hypothetical example, a 4% tax-exempt yield divided by 0.65 equals about 6.15% for a household facing a 35% marginal rate on comparable taxable interest. That result changes with the actual tax treatment, state rules, purchase price, fees, and the taxable alternative. The comparison must also account for different credit and interest-rate risk. A high tax bracket alone does not make every municipal bond attractive.

Price and call terms can defeat the coupon story

Bond prices generally move opposite to market interest rates. A bond bought at a premium can lose value as it approaches repayment at par, even while it pays an attractive coupon. A household that may need to sell early faces the current market price. The scheduled maturity date does not remove that interim price risk. A bond fund maintains a portfolio of maturities and trades, so its share price can move without a promised maturity value for one share.

Many municipal bonds can be called before maturity. Investor.gov explains that an issuer may redeem a callable bond when refinancing becomes favorable, ending its interest payments. A buyer who paid above par can receive a lower return if the bond is called. The MSRB's investor guide defines yield to worst as the lowest calculated yield among relevant redemption outcomes. Review the call schedule and yield to worst alongside yield to maturity. A quoted coupon is the contractual payment rate on face value. The buyer’s return depends on the price paid and redemption timing.

The repayment source deserves its own file

A general obligation bond and a revenue bond can draw on different payment sources. The SEC advises investors to read the official statement, identify the actual obligor, and examine the revenues pledged to repay a bond. A hospital, utility, transportation project, or local tax base has its own exposure. A rating is a useful signal at a point in time, but it does not replace review of the issuer's finances, covenants, and later disclosures. Credit conditions can change during a long holding period.

The Municipal Securities Rulemaking Board's EMMA system provides official statements, continuing disclosures, and reported trade prices. Check the precise CUSIP and issue before comparing a dealer quote with market trades. Sparse trading can make an exit costly even if the issuer continues paying. Transaction size, spread, and available buyers matter when the household might need the money before maturity.

A purchase test for the current market

Define the cash need first. Money reserved for near-term spending may not tolerate a long bond's price movement or uncertain sale terms. For a longer holding period, compare a municipal bond's purchase price, yield to worst, call dates, credit exposure, and expected after-tax cash flow against a Treasury or taxable alternative of similar duration. Document whether the comparison is for an individual bond held to maturity, a ladder with staggered maturities, or a fund with ongoing turnover. Those are different liquidity and price arrangements.

Use the actual tax return and the bond's offering documents. A high quoted yield can reflect a cheap price, weak credit, a call structure, or a thin market. The decision becomes defensible when the tax advantage remains after those terms are visible. If the advantage depends on an assumed sale price or an unverified state exemption, the household has not yet measured it.
