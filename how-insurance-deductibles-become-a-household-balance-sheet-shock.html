How Insurance Deductibles Become a Household Balance-Sheet Shock

A deductible is a contract term until somebody needs care. Then it becomes a cash demand with a due date.

One medical event · two financial pressures · a test for usable reserves

Health insurance limits exposure, but it does not make the first part of a medical bill disappear. In the 2025 KFF Employer Health Benefits Survey, 88 percent of covered workers with single coverage were enrolled in a plan with a general annual deductible. Among workers whose plans had one, the average deductible for single coverage was $1,886. Thirty-four percent of covered workers faced a single-coverage deductible of at least $2,000.

Those figures describe plan design, not a household's ability to pay. The financial question is whether the bill can be met on the timetable of treatment without revolving credit, a retirement withdrawal, a forced asset sale, or a loan against the house. Net worth can look substantial while cash available this week is thin.

Insurance limits the loss. Liquidity determines whether the household can finance the first part of it.

The shock is larger than the deductible

A deductible is the amount a patient pays for certain covered services before the plan begins paying, subject to the plan's rules. It is not the same as the out-of-pocket maximum. Copayments and coinsurance may continue after the deductible has been met. Premiums, uncovered services, some out-of-network care, and charges above an insurer's allowed amount may not count toward the maximum.

The medical event can also reduce income. Time away from work, unpaid leave, travel, caregiving, and follow-up visits can arrive beside the bill. A household that plans only for the deductible may therefore understate the full cash demand.

Run a dated cash-flow test

Start with the household's actual summary of benefits and coverage. Record the individual and family deductibles, the family structure of those deductibles, the coinsurance rules, and the out-of-pocket maximum. Then separate money that can be used within days from wealth that requires a sale, loan, penalty, or long settlement period.

Near-term medical exposureDeductible plus likely coinsurance and copaymentsUsing the deductible as the maximum possible bill
Accessible cashCash and insured deposits available within daysCounting home equity or retirement assets as immediate cash
Essential monthly spendingHousing, food, transport, premiums, debt payments, and careAssuming normal spending stops during recovery
Income interruptionUnpaid leave, reduced hours, or caregiving timeTesting the bill but not the lost income

The output is a financing gap, not a generic rule of thumb:

Near-term medical costs + essential spending + income lost during recovery − cash available within days = the amount that would need financing.

Consider an illustrative household with a $3,000 family deductible, another $1,000 of plausible coinsurance and copayments, $4,500 of essential monthly spending, and $1,500 of income lost during recovery. If $6,000 is accessible within days, the test exposes a $4,000 financing gap. The example is not a reserve target. It shows why one annual deductible number is not enough.

An HSA helps, but it is not the whole plan

A funded health savings account can create a dedicated, tax-advantaged source for eligible medical expenses. Employer contributions can materially reduce the household's effective exposure. KFF's survey also shows that many employer contributions do not fully cover the deductible, and the account may not cover the income interruption or ordinary bills that continue during recovery.

The account's investment policy matters. Money intended for near-term medical claims should not be treated as fully available if it is exposed to market losses or requires time to settle. The same distinction applies to a credit line. Borrowing capacity may provide a backstop, but it is not equivalent to a reserve because the medical event leaves a repayment obligation behind.

Build the reserve in layers

The first layer is immediate cash for routine deductibles, copayments, and a brief disruption. The second is a broader emergency reserve for a longer income gap. Insurance is the third layer, limiting losses that would overwhelm ordinary savings. The layers work together. None should be assumed to perform the job of all three.

Recheck the test at open enrollment, after a job change, when adding a dependent, and after a material change in savings or debt. Deductibles reset. Family rules differ. A plan that fit last year may produce a different financing gap this year.

Connect the number to the rest of the balance sheet
