Is Your Joint Bank Account Fully Insured?

For qualifying joint accounts, the FDIC generally insures each co-owner up to $250,000 for that person’s combined interests in all joint accounts at the same insured bank.

FDIC insurance · co-owners · ownership category · bank concentration

The account balance alone does not determine coverage. The relevant calculation is each person’s share across qualifying joint accounts at one FDIC-insured institution, separated from deposits in other ownership categories.

Two qualifying equal co-owners can generally receive up to $500,000 of joint-account coverage at one insured bank. That does not mean every account with two names qualifies, and it does not create a separate limit for each joint account.

Apply the per-owner rule

The FDIC combines a person’s interests in all qualifying joint accounts at the same insured depository institution. It assumes equal ownership unless the bank’s records clearly show otherwise. A couple with one $500,000 qualifying joint account therefore generally has $250,000 attributed to each owner and no uninsured amount in the joint-account category.

Confirm the account qualifies

The FDIC lists three core conditions: co-owners must be natural persons, each must have equal withdrawal rights, and each must personally sign the account signature card or the bank records must establish co-ownership. Electronic signatures can satisfy the signature requirement. Some negotiable instruments and certificates of deposit are exempt from the signature requirement while still needing to meet the other conditions.

Changing the order of names, using different Social Security numbers, or alternating “and” and “or” does not create a new insurance limit. The ownership facts and the bank matter.

Do not merge ownership categories

FDIC insurance categories are separate. A person’s qualifying joint-account coverage is distinct from coverage for that person’s single accounts, certain trust accounts, and some other ownership categories. But a joint account with payable-on-death beneficiaries is not insured as an ordinary joint account; the FDIC treats co-owned testamentary accounts under its trust-account rules.

Use the FDIC tool for complex households

Multiple owners, unequal interests, payable-on-death designations, business accounts, and a recent co-owner death can change the calculation. The FDIC provides Electronic Deposit Insurance Estimator, or EDIE, for depositors to model coverage.
