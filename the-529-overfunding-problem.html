LONGFORM · EDUCATION SAVINGS

The 529 Overfunding Problem

OCTOBER 12, 2026 · WEALTHMETER

A family can keep funding a 529 account after the likely education bill has stopped growing. The mismatch may remain hidden while the student is young, then surface when scholarships, a lower-cost school, or a changed plan leaves more money than qualified expenses.

The account is still the family's asset under its plan rules, and unused money has possible destinations. The issue is the tax treatment and flexibility of each destination. The IRS says tax-free 529 distributions depend on adjusted qualified education expenses. A balance alone does not measure how much can leave tax free.

Estimate the expense range before adding money

Start with the account balance and expected years until use. Then put a range around tuition, required fees, books, equipment, and any room and board that qualifies under the applicable rules. Separate expenses that a 529 can cover from a household's broader education budget. Scholarships and other tax-free assistance reduce the expenses available to support tax-free 529 distributions, as IRS Publication 970 explains.

Forecasts should cover several credible tuition and aid paths. A child may choose a different school, receive aid, attend part time, pursue an eligible credential, or skip a planned program. Investment returns can also diverge from an assumed rate. The decision to keep contributing should be reviewed against those branches and the household's other demands on cash.

What an unused balance can do

One path is to leave the funds for later qualified education. The timing can be longer than an undergraduate degree, depending on the beneficiary's plans and the program's terms. Another path is a change of beneficiary to a qualifying family member. The IRS says such a change generally has no federal income-tax consequence. Plan administration and state tax treatment still need checking before a transfer.

A 529-to-Roth IRA rollover offers a narrower path for the same beneficiary. IRS Publications 970 and 590-A require a direct trustee-to-trustee transfer, an account open for more than 15 years, and exclusion of contributions and related earnings from the most recent five years. The rollover shares the annual Roth IRA contribution limit with other Roth contributions and has a $35,000 lifetime ceiling. Those limits can leave a substantial unused balance in a long-held account.

The taxable withdrawal calculation

If a distribution exceeds adjusted qualified education expenses, the entire withdrawal is not automatically taxable. The IRS calculates the taxable share from the earnings portion associated with the nonqualified amount. A 10% additional federal tax generally applies to taxable earnings, although exceptions exist. For example, a scholarship can remove the additional tax on an amount within the scholarship limit; the earnings can still be included in income.

This distinction matters when comparing a deliberate taxable withdrawal with years of continued investment, account fees, and the chance of future qualified use. The family should obtain the plan's transaction details and check its state tax rules before treating the federal calculation as the total cost.

Set a contribution stop test

At each annual review, compare the existing balance plus plausible growth with low, central, and high qualified-cost estimates. Document what share of the bill another source is likely to pay. If the current balance already covers the central estimate, directing new savings to a more flexible account may preserve choices. That planning inference depends on the household's cost range and need for liquidity.

The question is how much education spending the household can support under several credible paths. A 529 can remain useful after the first student finishes, but the tax advantage depends on what the money ultimately pays for and who receives it.
