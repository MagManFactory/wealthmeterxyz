The Bottom Half Grew Fastest, and Still Holds 2.3%

The bottom half of U.S. households by net worth more than doubled its collective wealth between the end of 2019 and the second quarter of 2026. Its gain was the fastest in percentage terms among the Federal Reserve's five wealth groups. That percentile group held 2.3% of the total at the end of the period. Both facts belong in the headline because the size of the starting balance changes what a growth rate means.

Federal Reserve DFA · household wealth · debt · liquidity · financial resilience

The Federal Reserve's Distributional Financial Accounts, or DFA, estimate how household assets and liabilities are distributed. The latest file available for this article ends in 2026 Q2. It places the bottom 50% at $1.86 trillion in net worth in 2019 Q4 and $4.28 trillion in 2026 Q2, an increase of about $2.41 trillion or 129.5%. Their share of estimated household wealth moved from 1.73% to 2.30%. Those are aggregate, model-based estimates. They do not follow the same families over time, and they do not show that every household improved.

A 129.5% gain from $1.86 trillion added about $2.41 trillion. The top 0.1% gained about $14.51 trillion from a much larger base over the same period.

Percentage growth and dollar growth answer different questions

For a household with a small or negative starting balance, paying down a debt can produce a striking percentage change in net worth. A rise from $1,000 to $2,000 is a 100% gain and leaves only $2,000 of buffer. At the group level, the bottom half's 2.30-fold rise exceeded the top 0.1%'s 2.09-fold rise. The top group's net worth moved from $13.36 trillion to $27.87 trillion. The starting balances explain why those dollar gains differ so sharply.

The DFA is built by allocating Financial Accounts totals using the Survey of Consumer Finances and models for quarters between and after survey waves. The Fed says the period after the 2022 survey is extrapolated. Recent figures therefore have more model dependence than direct survey observations. Group membership also changes as households age, earn, inherit, buy homes, or take on debt. A change in a percentile group's total cannot be read as a panel study of particular households.

The balance sheet became less leveraged

In the Fed's 2026 Q2 file, the bottom half held about $10.27 trillion of assets and owed about $5.99 trillion. Liabilities equaled 58.4% of assets. The ratio was about 72.9% in 2019 Q4, calculated from the same DFA level file. A lower aggregate debt-to-asset ratio can indicate more room to absorb a decline in asset values. It cannot tell a reader whether their own payment burden is manageable, because the group contains renters, owners, young households, and households with negative net worth.

Asset form matters. Real estate accounted for about 46.9% of the group's assets in 2026 Q2, consumer durables for 21.1%, defined contribution pension entitlements for about 8.0%, and corporate equities and mutual fund shares held outside those pensions for about 3.6%. A vehicle can be valuable and essential for work while offering little spendable reserve. Home equity can rise while monthly cash remains tight. Retirement accounts can grow while early withdrawals impose tax or penalty costs. The DFA does not show each household's cash balance or the time required to turn an asset into money.

Averages are a poor substitute for a household test

The bottom-half group's aggregate net worth divided by its estimated 67.6 million households is about $63,000. That average is not the median of those households. It can be influenced by households near the 50th percentile and says little about a household with student debt, a mortgage, or unstable income. The top 0.1% average exceeds $200 million in the same file, but that comparison does not identify an actionable savings target for a family trying to withstand a job loss.

For that family, the immediate test is whether essential bills can be paid while income is interrupted or a major expense arrives. Add liquid assets that can be used without selling a home or borrowing, subtract obligations due soon, and compare the remainder with recurring essential spending. WealthMeter's runway tool helps make the timing visible. The net worth tool supplies the stock measure; both are needed because a positive net-worth number can coexist with a short cash runway.

What the 2.3% share can and cannot establish

The share shows that the bottom half held a small portion of U.S. household wealth even after large percentage growth. It does not isolate the effects of relief payments, wage gains, housing prices, debt repayment, or market movements. The DFA series measures the result of those forces in modeled balance sheets; causal attribution requires other evidence. The 2026 Q2 household total across the five DFA groups is about $185.65 trillion. The broader Financial Accounts household-and-nonprofit total is a different measure and should not be substituted into this percentage.

A reader can use the headline as context, then test their own position with specific questions. How much of net worth is accessible within a month? Which liabilities have payments that rise with rates? How long can essential spending continue after an income stop? Which next milestone would change that answer: clearing high-cost debt, building a liquid reserve, or increasing durable earnings? A larger percentile share would signal distributional change. Household resilience still depends on the form, timing, and obligations behind each balance sheet.
